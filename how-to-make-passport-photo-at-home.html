How to Make a Passport-Size Photo at Home with Your Phone

A photo studio charges for something your phone can do in five minutes — if you respect the rules. Passport and ID photos are rejected for surprisingly small reasons: the wrong proportions, a shadow on the face, a face that fills too little of the frame. This guide walks through the whole job at home: the size rules, taking a photo that passes, cropping it to exactly 35 × 45 mm, and printing it properly.

The size rules, in plain numbers

Most countries — including Pakistan and India — and most ID, visa and job-application systems use the 35 mm × 45 mm size (about 1.38 × 1.77 inches). For digital files, that shape at print quality (300 DPI) is 413 × 531 pixels. Within the frame, your head and shoulders should fill most of the picture, with your face roughly centred and a small gap above your head. The important exception: the United States uses a square 2 × 2 inch photo instead. Always read your specific form's instructions first — if it says 35 × 45 mm, everything in this guide applies exactly.

Step 1: Take a photo that can pass

  • Background: stand in front of a plain, light-coloured wall. No furniture, doors, patterns or other people behind you. Most official rules want a plain white or off-white background.
  • Light: face a window in daylight, with the light falling evenly on your face. Avoid a bright window behind you (your face goes dark), harsh ceiling light (deep eye shadows) and direct flash (red-eye and shiny skin).
  • Camera position: hold the phone at eye level, about an arm's length plus a step away. A selfie taken very close distorts the face — the nose looks larger and the ears disappear. Better: prop the phone up or ask someone to take it, and include your head, neck and shoulders with space around them. You will crop in step 2, so extra room is good.
  • Pose and expression: face the camera directly, head straight (not tilted), eyes open and looking at the lens, mouth closed, neutral expression. Remove sunglasses and caps; most countries allow religious head coverings. Many countries now prefer photos without glasses — check your form, and when in doubt, take the photo without them.
  • Freshness: most rules require a photo taken within the last six months that looks like you today. Use a new photo, with no beauty filters.

Step 2: Crop it to exactly 35 × 45 mm

  1. Open the Passport Size Photo Maker and choose the photo you just took.
  2. Use the Zoom slider until your head and shoulders fill the frame the way official samples show — face large, a small gap above the head, shoulders visible at the bottom.
  3. Use the move sliders to centre your face left-to-right. Your nose should sit on the imaginary centre line of the frame.
  4. Download. The file is saved at 413 × 531 pixels — exactly the 35 × 45 mm shape at 300 DPI print resolution.

One honest limitation: the maker places your photo on a white canvas but cannot remove a busy background from the photo itself. That is why step 1 insists on a plain wall — two minutes of preparation beats any editing afterwards.

Step 3: If the form also demands a file size

Many online portals pair the size rule with a weight rule — "35 × 45 mm, under 50 KB" is a typical combination. After downloading your cropped photo, run it through the Compress Image to KB tool with the exact target from your form. The shape stays 35 × 45 mm; only the file weight changes. (Our exact-KB guide explains that process in full.)

Step 4: Printing, if you need paper copies

Studios and print shops usually print passport photos on a standard 4 × 6 inch sheet, which fits 8 copies of a 35 × 45 mm photo. You can hand the shop your downloaded file — every studio recognises this size — or print at home: place copies of the 413 × 531 file on a 4 × 6 layout at 300 DPI, print on glossy photo paper at 100% scale (never "fit to page", which changes the size), and cut carefully along the edges. Measure one finished photo with a ruler: it should be exactly 35 mm wide and 45 mm tall.

The mistakes that get photos rejected

  • Wrong proportions — a square selfie or a full-length photo where 35 × 45 mm is demanded.
  • Face too small — the "head and shoulders" rule fails when the face is a small part of the frame. Zoom in.
  • Shadows and red-eye — the two most common studio-rejection reasons, both avoided by window light.
  • Busy or dark background — take the original against a plain light wall.
  • Old or filtered photos — heavy filters change how you look, and offices can and do refuse them.
  • Head coverings and glasses that hide the eyes — follow your form's exact wording.

A quick worked example

Your raw photo is 3,024 × 4,032 pixels — tall, shoulders-down, with a doorway behind your left shoulder. Retake it against the white bedroom wall, facing the window. Upload the new photo, zoom to about 140% so the face fills the frame, nudge it slightly down to leave a small gap above the head, and download: a 413 × 531 pixel, form-shaped photo. The form also wants under 50 KB, so one pass through the exact-KB tool finishes the job — total time, about five minutes, total cost, zero.
