Word Counter — live word & character count

For essays, articles, assignments and social media posts with limits.

What this counter measures

Words (groups of characters separated by spaces), characters with and without spaces — important for forms and platforms with character limits — plus sentences, paragraphs, and how long the text takes to read silently (estimated at an average 200 words per minute) and to speak aloud (about 130 words per minute — handy for speeches and video scripts). Everything updates live as you type or paste, so you can trim or expand your text while watching the numbers move.

How the counts are calculated

Words are counted the way Microsoft Word and Google Docs count them: the text is split wherever there is a space, and each piece counts as one word — so "well-known" counts as one word, and "don't" counts as one word. The character count includes everything you typed, spaces and punctuation too; the second count removes spaces only, which is the version some exam forms and applications ask for. Sentences are detected from full stops, question marks and exclamation marks; paragraphs are blocks of text separated by a blank line. Reading and speaking times are estimates — fast readers and presenters will differ, but the averages are the ones commonly used in publishing and broadcasting.

A worked example

Paste in a 650-word essay and the counter shows roughly: 650 words, about 3,900 characters with spaces and 3,300 without, perhaps 40 sentences in 6 paragraphs — reading time about 3 minutes, speaking time about 5 minutes. That speaking figure is exactly how presenters and video creators plan: a 10-minute talk or video needs roughly 1,300 words of script, and a 1-minute short video needs about 130.

Common limits people check here

  • School & university essays (500 / 1,000 / 2,000 words)
  • Personal statements (~4,000 characters)
  • Video scripts (about 130 words = 1 minute of speech)
  • Articles and blog posts for SEO (1,000+ words)
  • CVs and cover letters (one page ≈ 400–500 words)
  • Social posts with hard character limits

Reaching a target without wrecking your writing

Too long? Cut in this order: repeated points first, then long examples where a short one works, then filler phrases ("it is important to note that", "due to the fact that"). Never fix an over-length essay by shrinking the font or deleting whole arguments. Too short? Add substance, not padding: a concrete example, a short quotation with a comment on it, or the answer to an obvious objection. Graders recognise padding instantly, and it usually lowers the mark it was meant to protect.

Common counting mistakes

  • Hyphenated words and numbers: "twenty-one" and "1,000" each count as one word here and in Word — if your institution counts differently, the difference is small but worth knowing at a strict limit.
  • Character limits that include spaces: most do. If a form rejects your text as too long, check the with-spaces count — it is almost always the larger one that applies.
  • References and titles: some assignments include them in the limit, others exclude them. The rules of your specific assignment decide — paste text with and without them to see both figures.

Sentence length — the hidden quality number

Divide your word count by your sentence count and you get the average sentence length, a rough but honest readability gauge. Most clear non-fiction averages 15–20 words per sentence; academic writing runs longer, and anything averaging above 25 usually means sentences that readers must re-read — if your text feels "heavy", this number is often why, and splitting the longest sentences is the fastest fix. The opposite extreme also shows up here: an average under 10 words per sentence reads as choppy and abrupt, fine for instructions and children's books but tiring in an essay. For spoken work — speeches, presentations, video scripts — shorter wins again, because a listener cannot rewind a sentence the way a reader can.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How are words counted?

Words are groups of characters separated by spaces — the same method used by Microsoft Word and Google Docs. Hyphenated words like "well-known" count as one word.

Q: Why do I need characters without spaces?

Some forms and platforms (for example university personal statements and SMS) count characters, sometimes excluding spaces. Both counts are shown.

Q: How is speaking time calculated?

Average speech is about 130 words per minute, so a 650-word script is roughly a 5-minute talk or video. Reading time uses about 200 words per minute, the common silent-reading average.

Q: Is my text saved or sent anywhere?

No. Counting happens in your browser; your text never leaves your device and is gone when you close the page.

Q: Does the count include my title and references?

It includes everything in the box. If your limit excludes titles or reference lists, delete them from the pasted text to see the body-only count, then add them back in your document.

Q: How many words is a 5-minute video script?

About 650 words at the average speaking speed of 130 words per minute. For a 1-minute video, aim for roughly 130 words.

Q: How long should a blog post or article be?

There is no magic number, but posts under 300 words rarely rank or satisfy a reader, 800–1,500 words suits most how-to topics, and detailed guides often run 2,000+. Match the length to the question being answered — and check the reading time above: most visitors give an article two to four minutes, which is 400–800 words.
