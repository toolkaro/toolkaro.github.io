Resize, compress and crop — three different jobs

People use one word — "make it smaller" — for three different operations, and using the wrong one ruins photos. Resizing changes the pixel dimensions: a 4000 × 3000 photo becomes 1200 × 900, keeping the whole picture. Compressing keeps the dimensions and shrinks the file size by storing less detail — our Image Compressor does that, and our Compress Image to KB tool hunts for one exact file size. Cropping cuts away part of the picture to change its shape. This page resizes. If a website rejects your photo for being too many kilobytes, you need compression, not resizing; if it rejects it for being the wrong dimensions ("must be 1080 × 1080"), you need this page.

How to resize a photo

  1. Upload your photo — the page shows its current dimensions.
  2. Choose Exact pixels and type the width and height, or choose Percentage and type a scale like 50%.
  3. Keep the aspect ratio lock on unless you deliberately want to change the shape — with it on, typing the width fills in the height automatically and the picture cannot stretch.
  4. Press Resize, preview the result, and download it.

A worked example

A phone photo is 4000 × 3000 pixels and a shop listing wants images no wider than 1200 pixels. Type 1200 as the width with the lock on: the height fills in as 900 (because 4000:3000 is the same shape as 1200:900), and the resized photo has 1200 × 900 = 1.08 million pixels instead of 12 million — roughly a tenth of the pixels, which also makes the file far smaller. The percentage route gives the same answer another way: 30% of 4000 is 1200, and 30% of 3000 is 900. Both routes keep the whole picture; neither cuts anything off.

The sizes platforms actually ask for

Memorising one table saves repeated guessing: Instagram post — 1080 × 1080 (square); Instagram story — 1080 × 1920 (tall); Facebook link preview — 1200 × 630; YouTube thumbnail — 1280 × 720; WhatsApp profile — 640 × 640 works well; Etsy listing — 2000 × 2000 is recommended for zoom. The presets on this page cover the most-used four; for the rest, type the numbers. One caution about squares: forcing a wide photo into a square by typing 1080 × 1080 with the lock off stretches faces sideways. For a square result from a wide photo, crop first (our Passport Size Photo Maker shows the crop-and-centre pattern) and then resize, or accept the platform's own crop.

Does resizing lose quality?

Resizing down is safe: a 4000-pixel photo reduced to 1200 pixels still looks sharp on any screen, because screens show at most about 2000 pixels wide anyway. The detail removed was invisible at display size. Resizing up is the opposite: enlarging a 600-pixel photo to 2000 pixels invents pixels that were never there, so the result goes soft — acceptable for a small thumbnail, disappointing as a large print. The rule is simple: resize down freely, resize up only when you must, and never resize the same photo up and down repeatedly — each pass softens it a little more. Keep the original safe and always resize from it.

Common mistakes

  • Turning the lock off by accident. A stretched photo is the giveaway of a rushed listing. If faces look wide or tall, the lock was off.
  • Resizing instead of compressing. A 1200 × 900 photo saved at full quality can still be over a form's KB limit. After resizing to the right dimensions, run it through the Image Compressor or Compress to KB tool if a file-size limit remains.
  • Losing transparency. A logo with a transparent background stays transparent only as PNG. Downloading it as JPG puts it on a white background. This tool keeps PNG when you choose "Keep original format" on a PNG upload.
  • Upscaling for print. A phone photo printed at 6 × 4 inches needs about 1800 × 1200 pixels. A 640-pixel WhatsApp copy upscaled to that size will print soft — print from the original instead.

Private by design

Like every tool on this site, resizing happens entirely in your browser on your own device. Your photo is never uploaded to any server, which matters for ID photos, product shots before launch, and children's pictures. Resizing here also strips the hidden EXIF data phone cameras embed — including GPS location — so a resized copy is safer to post publicly than the original straight from the camera.

What size should you actually choose?

The right size depends on where the picture will live. Thumbnails and avatars: 150–400 pixels wide is plenty — anything larger is wasted bytes on a tiny display. Blog and shop images: 1200 pixels wide is the sweet spot; it looks sharp on laptops and phones without slowing the page. Full-screen headers: 1920 pixels wide matches most monitors. Prints: work backwards from paper — a 6 × 4 inch print at 300 DPI needs 1800 × 1200 pixels, so resize to at least that, from the original. A useful rule of thumb for file size after resizing: a 1200-pixel-wide JPG at good quality usually lands between 150 and 400 KB depending on how detailed the scene is. If the result is still over a limit after resizing to the right dimensions, that is a compression job — run it through the Image Compressor or Compress to KB tool as a second step, rather than shrinking the dimensions further and making the picture needlessly small.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is the difference between resize and compress?

Resizing changes pixel dimensions (4000×3000 becomes 1200×900). Compressing keeps the dimensions and shrinks the file size. If a form rejects your photo for kilobytes, compress it; if it demands exact dimensions, resize it.

Q: Will my photo stretch?

Not with the aspect ratio lock on — it fills in the second dimension automatically so the shape never changes. Turn it off only if you deliberately want a different shape.

Q: What size should I use for Instagram?

1080 × 1080 for a square post, 1080 × 1920 for a story. The presets on this page cover Instagram posts, Facebook link images (1200 × 630) and YouTube thumbnails (1280 × 720).

Q: Can I make a small photo bigger?

Yes, with a percentage above 100% — but upscaling invents pixels, so the result goes soft. Resize down freely; resize up only when you must, and always from the original.

Q: Does resizing keep PNG transparency?

Yes, when you keep the original format on a PNG upload, or choose PNG output. Downloading as JPG puts the image on a white background.

Q: Is my photo uploaded anywhere?

No. Resizing runs entirely in your browser on your own device, and the resized copy also drops the camera's hidden location data.
