The three percentage formulas

Almost every percentage question in daily life is one of three shapes, and this page has one mode for each:

1. Percentage of a number: (percent ÷ 100) × number. Example: 20% of 500 = 0.20 × 500 = 100.

2. One number as a percent of another: (first ÷ second) × 100. Example: 45 ÷ 200 = 0.225, so 45 is 22.5% of 200.

3. Percentage change: ((new − old) ÷ old) × 100. A positive result is an increase, a negative result is a decrease. Example: from 100 to 125 is (25 ÷ 100) × 100 = a 25% increase.

Worked examples from everyday life

Shopping discount. A jacket costs 4,000 and is marked "30% off". Mode 1: 30% of 4,000 = 1,200 saved, so you pay 2,800. If the shop then adds a further 10% off at the till, that is 10% of 2,800 = 280 more — a total price of 2,520, which is not the same as 40% off the original.

Salary raise. Your salary rises from 60,000 to 69,000. Mode 3: (69,000 − 60,000) ÷ 60,000 × 100 = a 15% increase — the number to compare when another employer offers "10% more than you earn now".

Exam score. You scored 171 out of 180. Mode 2: 171 ÷ 180 × 100 = 95%. (For a full multi-subject result, use our Marks Percentage Calculator.)

Common mistakes that change the answer

  • Adding percentages directly. A 30% discount followed by a 10% discount is 37% off in total, not 40%, because the second discount applies to the already-reduced price.
  • Confusing percent with percentage points. If an interest rate moves from 10% to 12%, that is a rise of 2 percentage points — but a 20% increase in the rate itself. News reports mix these up constantly.
  • Dividing by the wrong number in change problems. Percentage change is always divided by the old (starting) value, never the new one.
  • Thinking a 50% loss needs a 50% gain to undo. If 200 falls 50% to 100, getting back to 200 needs a 100% gain. Losses and gains are not symmetrical.

A quick mental shortcut

To estimate any percentage in your head: find 10% first (just move the decimal point one place left), then build from it. 10% of 4,000 is 400, so 30% is 400 × 3 = 1,200, and 5% is half of 10% = 200. It will not replace the calculator for exact work, but it catches mistakes when a shop's "discount" does not feel right.

Reverse percentages — finding the original number

A fourth question appears constantly in real life, and it needs the formulas turned around: you know the result after a percentage was applied, and you want the original. If a price after a 20% discount is 2,400, the original was 2,400 ÷ 0.80 = 3,000 — you divide by (1 − 20/100). If a salary after a 15% raise is 69,000, the old salary was 69,000 ÷ 1.15 = 60,000 — divide by (1 + 15/100). The classic error is to add or subtract the percentage of the new number instead: 20% of 2,400 is 480, and 2,400 + 480 = 2,880, which is wrong by 120. Reverse checks like these are how you catch an inflated "before" price in a sale, or confirm that a pay slip applied the raise you were promised.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How do I find a percentage of a number?

Divide the percent by 100 and multiply by the number. For example, 20% of 500 = 0.20 × 500 = 100. Mode 1 above does this for you.

Q: How is percentage increase calculated?

Subtract the old value from the new value, divide by the old value, and multiply by 100. Mode 3 does this and labels the result as an increase or decrease.

Q: Can this calculate discounts?

Yes — use mode 1: a 30% discount on 2,000 means you save 600, so you pay 1,400.

Q: Is it free?

Yes, free and unlimited, with no sign-up.

Q: What is the difference between percent and percentage points?

Percent is a relative change; percentage points are the plain difference between two percentages. A rate going from 10% to 12% rose by 2 percentage points, which is a 20% relative increase.

Q: A price fell 20% then rose 20%. Is it back to the original?

No. Falling 20% from 100 gives 80; rising 20% from 80 gives 96. Percentage changes multiply rather than add, and each one is based on a different starting value.

Q: How do I find the original price before a discount?

Divide the price you paid by (1 − discount/100). Paid 2,400 after a 20% discount? 2,400 ÷ 0.80 = 3,000 was the original price. Adding 20% of the new price instead gives the wrong answer, because the discount was taken from the old, larger number.

Q: How do I calculate a percentage in my head quickly?

Find 10% first — move the decimal point one place left — then build up: double it for 20%, halve it for 5%, add pieces together for awkward figures like 35% (10% × 3 + 5%). It is an estimate, but it catches most till and billing errors on the spot.
