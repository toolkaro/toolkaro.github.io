Compound Interest Calculator — how savings grow over time

Your money earning interest, and then interest on the interest — the effect that makes time your biggest asset.

Simple interest vs compound interest

Simple interest pays only on the money you started with. Compound interest pays on your starting money plus all the interest already earned — interest on interest. The difference looks small in year one and enormous by year ten, which is why compounding is called the most powerful force in saving: time does more work than the rate does. A bank savings account, a prize-bond-free deposit scheme, a mutual fund or a business reinvesting its profit all grow by compounding; a loan you do not repay grows the same way against you, which is why the same maths appears on both sides of money.

The compound interest formula

Final amount A = P × (1 + r/n)nt, where P is the starting amount, r is the yearly rate as a decimal, n is how many times a year interest is added, and t is the number of years. With yearly compounding (n = 1) the formula shortens to A = P × (1 + r)t. This calculator applies the full formula and also shows the growth year by year, so you can see the curve bending upwards — the later years add far more than the early ones, because each year's interest is calculated on a bigger balance.

A worked example

Start with Rs 100,000 at 12% per year, compounded yearly, for 5 years. Year 1: 100,000 × 1.12 = 112,000. Year 2: 112,000 × 1.12 = 125,440. Year 3: 140,493. Year 4: 157,352. Year 5: 176,234. Total profit: Rs 76,234. Compare simple interest on the same deal: 12% of 100,000 is 12,000 a year, five times = Rs 60,000 profit, final Rs 160,000. Compounding added an extra Rs 16,234 for doing nothing extra — and the gap widens every year after that. With monthly compounding instead of yearly, the same 5 years give about Rs 181,670, because interest starts earning its own interest twelve times a year instead of once.

The Rule of 72 — doubling time in your head

Divide 72 by the yearly rate and you get roughly how many years it takes money to double. At 12%, 72 ÷ 12 = 6 years. At 9%, about 8 years. At 6%, about 12 years. It is an approximation, but a useful one: it turns an abstract rate into a sentence you can feel — "my money doubles every six years at this rate" — and it makes the cost of waiting visible. Starting five years later at 12% does not just lose five years of interest; it loses the doubling those five years would have produced on everything after them.

What compounding frequency really does

More frequent compounding always helps, but by less than people expect. On Rs 100,000 at 12% for 5 years: yearly gives Rs 176,234; half-yearly about Rs 179,085; quarterly about Rs 180,611; monthly about Rs 181,670. The jump from yearly to monthly is about Rs 5,400 — real money, but small next to the Rs 76,234 that time and rate produced. The lesson: do not chase a scheme because it compounds daily if its rate is lower. A full percentage point of rate beats any compounding frequency on offer.

The other side: compounding debt

The same curve that grows savings grows unpaid debt. A credit-card balance or a loan left to roll up compounds against you at rates far higher than any savings account pays — which is why minimum payments barely move a card balance: most of each payment is interest, and the interest itself starts earning interest. If you have both savings earning 12% and debt costing 30%+, paying the debt first is mathematically better than saving, because no safe investment reliably beats the rate the debt charges you. Common mistakes

  • Forgetting tax on profit. Bank profit and fund gains are usually taxed (in Pakistan, withholding applies to profit on debt). The calculator shows gross growth; your net will be a little lower.
  • Comparing a monthly rate with a yearly rate. A "1% monthly" return is not 12% yearly — compounded, it is (1.01)12 − 1 = 12.68%. Always convert to the same period before comparing.
  • Ignoring inflation. If prices rise 10% a year and your money grows 12%, your real gain is only about 2%. Growth that merely matches inflation is standing still in what money buys.
  • Stopping the clock. Compounding rewards patience most of all. Withdrawing in year two throws away the steep part of the curve, which is always at the end.

The cost of starting late — a tale of two savers

The most famous illustration of compounding compares two savers at 10% a year. Saver A puts aside Rs 10,000 a year from age 25 to 35 — ten payments, Rs 100,000 in total — and then stops, leaving the money alone until 65. At 35 the pot is about Rs 159,374; left to compound for 30 more years it grows to roughly Rs 2,780,000. Saver B waits until 35 and then puts aside Rs 10,000 a year all the way to 65 — thirty payments, Rs 300,000 in total, three times what A contributed. B's pot at 65 is about Rs 1,645,000. A ends up with over a million rupees more despite contributing a third as much, purely because A's money had ten extra years to compound. The moral is not that saving late is pointless — B still turned 300,000 into 1.6 million — but that the earliest years are the most valuable ones you will ever have, and no later effort fully buys them back.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest pays only on the starting amount. Compound interest pays on the starting amount plus all interest already earned, so growth accelerates. On Rs 100,000 at 12% for 5 years, simple interest gives Rs 60,000 profit; yearly compounding gives Rs 76,234.

Q: How often should interest compound?

More often is better, but the effect is modest next to the rate itself: on the example above, monthly compounding beats yearly by only about Rs 5,400 over 5 years. A higher rate matters far more than a higher frequency.

Q: What is the Rule of 72?

Divide 72 by the yearly rate to estimate doubling time: at 12%, money roughly doubles in 6 years; at 9%, in 8 years. It is an approximation, not an exact formula.

Q: Does this include tax?

No — the figures are gross. Profit on bank deposits and funds is usually taxed, so your net return will be a little lower.

Q: Can I use this for a loan?

The same formula describes debt growing against you. For fixed monthly loan payments, use the EMI Calculator instead, which shows the monthly payment and total interest.

Q: Why does growth speed up in later years?

Because each year's interest is calculated on a larger balance. In the worked example, year 1 adds Rs 12,000 but year 5 adds about Rs 18,900 — the same 12% rate, applied to more money.
