Currency Converter — USD to PKR and world currencies

Live mid-market rates for the currencies Pakistanis check every day: dollar, riyal, dirham, pound and euro.

Rates are indicative mid-market rates from free public sources. Banks and exchange companies buy and sell at slightly different rates — open-market cash rates in Pakistan are usually Rs 1–3 above the interbank rate.

What this converter does

Enter an amount, pick the two currencies, and press Convert. The tool fetches the latest exchange rate from a free public rate source and shows you the converted amount plus the rate it used, for example "1 USD = 276.80 PKR". Everything happens in your browser; the rate comes from the source live each time you convert, so the number is as fresh as the source itself. If the rate source cannot be reached — for example on a weak connection — the tool says so plainly instead of inventing a rate, because a stale or guessed rate is worse than no rate when money is involved.

Mid-market, interbank and open-market — three rates, three meanings

Newspapers and converters quote the mid-market rate: the halfway point between what buyers and sellers will pay, roughly what banks trade among themselves (the interbank rate). It is the honest reference number, and it is what this tool shows. But it is not the rate you personally get. When you walk into an exchange company with cash, you get the open-market rate, which in Pakistan is usually Rs 1–3 higher per dollar than the interbank rate — the shop's spread covers its costs, demand and risk. Banks and remittance apps convert closer to the interbank rate, sometimes with a small premium added by government incentive schemes. So if this tool says 100 dollars = Rs 27,680 at the mid-market rate, expect a cash shop to hand over a little less, and a bank transfer to land a little closer to the quoted figure. Always check the rate your own provider quotes on the day before sending money — the headline figure is a guide, not a promise.

A worked example

Suppose the live rate is 1 USD = 276.80 PKR (the interbank level around 25 October 2026). Converting 100 USD gives Rs 27,680 at the mid-market rate. Converting the other way, Rs 10,000 divided by 276.80 gives about $36.13. A family member sending 500 dollars home would see 500 × 276.80 = Rs 138,400 before any provider spread or fee. Small rate moves add up at these sizes: a shift of just Rs 2 per dollar changes a 500-dollar remittance by Rs 1,000 — which is why people who receive money from abroad check the rate on the day they collect, not the day it was sent.

Why the rupee rate moves

The rupee's value against the dollar is set by supply and demand for dollars: remittances from overseas Pakistanis, export earnings, import payments (especially oil), foreign loans and repayments, and the State Bank of Pakistan's own reserves and policy. When dollars flow in strongly — a good remittance month, for example — the rupee tends to firm up; when imports surge or reserves fall, it tends to weaken. Big moves usually come with news: an IMF programme review, a change in the policy interest rate, or a jump in world oil prices. Day-to-day moves of a rupee or two are normal noise; the trend over months is what matters for anyone planning a large transfer, a fee payment abroad, or a purchase priced in dollars.

The currencies Pakistanis check most

US dollar (USD) is the reference for almost everything — imports, the PTA tax on phones (customs values phones in dollars and converts at the State Bank rate on the day of assessment), and overseas fees. Saudi riyal (SAR) and UAE dirham (AED) matter because most overseas Pakistanis work in Saudi Arabia and the Gulf; both are pegged to the dollar, so they move against the rupee almost exactly as the dollar does. British pound (GBP) matters for the large UK-Pakistani community and for students. Euro (EUR) covers Europe. All of them are in this converter, in both directions.

Common mistakes with converters

  • Treating the mid-market rate as cash in hand. The shop or app will quote its own rate, always a little worse than mid-market. Budget for the spread.
  • Forgetting fees. Some services advertise a great rate and charge a fee on top; others bake the fee into a worse rate. Compare the final rupees received, not the headline rate.
  • Using an old screenshot. A rate from last week is history. Rates move daily — convert fresh on the day you act.
  • Mixing up the direction. "USD to PKR" and "PKR to USD" are different calculations. The Swap button here flips them so you cannot get the pair backwards.

Is this rate official?

No single converter is "the" official rate. The State Bank of Pakistan publishes an interbank closing rate each working day; exchange companies publish their own buy/sell boards; Google and XE show mid-market estimates. They normally sit within a rupee or two of each other. This tool's rate comes from free public sources that track the same market, and it is labelled indicative for exactly that reason: it is the right number for planning and comparing, and your provider's quote on the day is the right number for the actual transaction.

Cross-checking a rate before you act

For a small conversion, one converter is enough. For a large transfer — a semester fee, a vehicle purchase, a house payment from abroad — cross-check two independent sources before you commit: the State Bank's published interbank closing, your own bank's board rate, and one converter like this one. If all three sit within a rupee or two, the market is calm and any of them is a fair guide. If they disagree by much more, something is moving fast — a policy announcement, a reserves update, a sudden oil-price jump — and waiting a day, or asking your provider to lock a rate, can be worth more than the conversion itself. Never accept a rate quoted only inside a chat message or a social-media post: a rate you cannot verify on an independent source is not a rate, it is a claim.
