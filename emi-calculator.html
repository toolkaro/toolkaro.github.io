EMI Calculator — monthly loan payment

For bike, car, personal and business loans — know the real cost before you sign.

What is EMI?

EMI (Equated Monthly Installment) is the fixed amount you pay every month until a loan is finished. The payment stays the same every month, but its ingredients change: in the early months most of each payment is interest, and only a small part reduces what you borrowed (the principal). As the balance falls, the interest portion shrinks and the principal portion grows, so the loan is cleared faster and faster towards the end. This calculator shows the EMI, the total interest over the whole loan, and the total amount you will hand over — the three numbers that decide whether a loan is genuinely affordable.

The EMI formula

EMI = P × r × (1+r)n ÷ ((1+r)n − 1), where P is the loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate (yearly rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100), and n is the number of months. Banks use this same reducing-balance formula for personal, car, bike and home loans. If the rate is zero, the formula is simply P ÷ n. This calculator applies the formula exactly, then multiplies the EMI by the number of months to give you the honest totals.

A worked example

You borrow 500,000 at 18% per year for 36 months. The monthly rate r is 18 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.015. The formula gives an EMI of about 18,076 per month. Over 36 months you pay about 650,700 in total — of which roughly 150,700 is interest, nearly a third of the amount you borrowed. Now stretch the same loan to 60 months: the EMI drops to about 12,697, which feels easier — but the total interest climbs to roughly 261,800. That single comparison is why the duration box in this calculator matters more than the EMI itself.

How duration and rate change what you pay

  • Shorter duration: higher monthly payment, dramatically less total interest.
  • Lower rate: even 2% less interest saves a large sum on a big or long loan — negotiating the rate, or improving the credit profile behind it, is real money.
  • Bigger down payment: borrowing less beats every other saving. Every amount you pay upfront is an amount that never accrues interest.

Before taking any loan

  • Compare the total interest, not just the monthly payment — a longer duration means a smaller EMI but much more interest overall.
  • Ask about processing fees, documentation charges, insurance bundled into the loan, and early-repayment penalties — none of these are inside the EMI formula.
  • Check whether the quoted rate is a reducing-balance rate (used here and by banks for standard loans) or a flat rate charged on the original amount for the whole term — a "12% flat" dealer loan can cost more than an 18% reducing-balance bank loan.
  • Keep total monthly installments comfortably below your monthly income, so one bad month cannot start a spiral of late fees.

Common mistakes

  • Judging a loan by the EMI alone and signing the longest duration on offer.
  • Forgetting that the first payments are mostly interest, so selling the vehicle or settling early in year one saves less than expected.
  • Comparing one bank's flat rate with another's reducing rate as if they were the same number.

The power of one early payment

The flip side of front-loaded interest is that extra money paid early does outsized work. Take the worked example above — 500,000 at 18% over 36 months, EMI about 18,076. After twelve payments your balance is about 362,074. Pay an extra 50,000 lump sum at that point (if your bank applies it to the principal with no penalty) and, keeping the same EMI, the loan finishes roughly four months early and you save about 20,000 in interest — because that 50,000 stops accruing interest for every remaining month, not just one. The later in the loan you pay extra, the less it saves, which is why a small early payment beats a heroic late one. Our guide derives the formula step by step: How EMI Is Calculated.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What does EMI include?

Each monthly payment includes interest for that month plus a part of the principal. Early payments are mostly interest; later payments are mostly principal.

Q: Does a longer loan duration save money?

It lowers the monthly payment but increases the total interest a lot. On a 500,000 loan at 18%, moving from 3 years to 5 years adds roughly 110,000 of extra interest. Compare the "Total interest" line before deciding.

Q: Can I use this for a car or bike loan?

Yes — any fixed-rate loan: personal, car, bike, home or business. Enter the amount you are actually borrowing (price minus your down payment).

Q: Is the result exact?

It matches the standard bank reducing-balance formula. Your bank may add fees or insurance, which would increase the real cost slightly.

Q: What is the difference between flat rate and reducing balance?

A reducing-balance rate charges interest only on what you still owe, so it falls every month. A flat rate charges interest on the original full amount for the whole term, which costs far more — a flat rate is roughly equivalent to a reducing rate nearly twice as high.

Q: What happens if I pay extra some months?

Extra payments reduce the principal early, which cuts the interest on every remaining month. Ask your bank whether they apply extra payments to the principal immediately and whether any early-settlement penalty applies.
