How to Create a Strong Password (and Stop Reusing Them)

Most accounts are not "hacked" by genius attackers breaking encryption. They fall to two dull tricks: guessing weak passwords with software, and reusing passwords leaked from other websites. Both tricks fail completely against a long, random, unique password. This guide explains how password cracking actually works, what strength really means, and a practical system for having a strong, different password on every account without memorising any of them.

How attackers guess passwords

Cracking software starts with lists: the billions of real passwords leaked from previous breaches, dictionary words in many languages, names, dates, and predictable patterns like a word plus a year plus a symbol ("Karachi@2024", "Ali#123"). It tries these at enormous speed — billions of combinations per second on modern hardware. Any password built from a name, a word, a date, or a pattern a human finds "clever" is in that first wave and falls quickly. What software cannot shortcut is randomness: a password with no word, no name and no pattern forces it to try combinations one by one, and the count multiplies with every character. That is the whole science: be unpredictable, and be long.

Length beats cleverness

Strength is measured in bits of entropy — roughly, how unpredictable each character is, multiplied by how many characters there are. An 8-character password chosen by a human might carry 25–30 bits of real unpredictability and can fall in minutes. A random 16-character password using upper and lower case letters, numbers and symbols carries about 104 bits: at billions of guesses per second, brute force would need centuries. Every extra character multiplies the work by the size of the character set (about 90 for the full set) — so adding four characters makes a password roughly 65 million times harder to crack. This is why "long and random" beats "short and complicated" every time, and why a generator that produces true randomness matters.

Creating a strong password, step by step

  1. Open the Password Generator. It builds passwords in your browser using your device's cryptographic random generator — the same secure randomness used for banking connections — and nothing is sent to or stored on any server.
  2. Set the length to at least 16 characters for important accounts (email, banking, your main social accounts). Use longer — 20 or more — where the site allows it.
  3. Tick uppercase, numbers and symbols. If a particular website rejects symbols (a few older ones do), untick them and add a few extra characters to keep the strength.
  4. Generate, copy, and paste the password directly into the site and into your password manager (see below) in the same moment — the generator keeps no copy, and neither should a sticky note.
  5. Repeat with a fresh, different password for every account. Never reuse, and never use "variations" of one password — once one leaks, the pattern is obvious.

The remembering problem — solved by not remembering

Nobody can memorise forty random 16-character passwords, and you are not meant to. A password manager — the one built into your browser or phone, or a dedicated app — stores them all in an encrypted vault opened by one strong master password (or your fingerprint/face). That single master password is the one password you do memorise, so make it excellent: a passphrase of five or six random words strung together is long, strong and genuinely memorable. Everything else lives in the manager, which also fills passwords in for you — a side benefit: it will refuse to fill your bank password on a fake lookalike site, because the address will not match.

Turn on two-factor authentication (2FA)

Even a perfect password can be phished or leaked by a careless website. Two-factor authentication adds a second step — a code from an authenticator app, or a prompt on your phone — so a stolen password alone is not enough to enter your account. Turn it on for email first (your email can reset every other password, so it is the master key to your digital life), then banking, then social accounts. Prefer an authenticator app over SMS codes where both are offered: SMS can be intercepted or the number reassigned, while an app code lives only on your device.

The mistakes that undo everything

  • Personal information — your name, children's or pets' names, birthdays, phone numbers. Attackers harvest these from social media and try them early.
  • One password everywhere — a single leak at a small, careless site hands attackers the key to your email and bank, because they automatically try leaked passwords on every major service (this is called credential stuffing, and it is the most common way accounts fall).
  • Sharing passwords by chat or email, or typing them on links sent by message. No real company ever asks for your password.
  • Short-but-fancy — eight characters with three symbols is still weak. Length first, variety second.
  • Delaying after a breach notice — if a service says its data leaked, change that password (and any account sharing it) the same day.

Your five-minute security reset

  1. Change your email password to a generated 16+ character password; store it in a password manager; turn on 2FA.
  2. Do the same for your banking and main social accounts.
  3. Work through your remaining accounts over the week, replacing every reused or weak password with a fresh generated one.
  4. Set a passphrase-style master password on the manager itself — and write it on paper kept somewhere safe at home, as the one backup that cannot be hacked.
