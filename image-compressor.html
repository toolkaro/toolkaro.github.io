Image Compressor — smaller files, same clear picture

Shrink photos for websites, WhatsApp, email and uploads — without losing visible quality.

When do you need to compress an image?

Modern phone cameras produce beautiful photos — and very large files, often 3–8 MB each. Big photos are slow to upload on mobile data, rejected by forms and websites with size limits, awkward to attach to email, and they quietly eat your phone storage. Compressing reduces the file size — often by 70–90% — while the picture still looks the same on screen, because most of what is removed is fine detail the human eye barely notices at normal viewing size.

How image compression works

This tool re-saves your photo as a JPG at the quality level you choose. JPG is a "lossy" format: when saving, it groups similar colours together and keeps less of the tiniest detail. At high quality settings the savings come almost free; at low settings you trade more visible softness for a much smaller file. The dimensions of your photo stay exactly the same — only the amount of stored detail changes. That is the difference from our Compress Image to KB tool, which hunts for one exact file size; this compressor instead lets you choose the quality yourself with a simple slider.

Quality vs size — choosing your setting

80% is the sweet spot for most photos: a big reduction with no visible change on a phone or laptop screen. Go lower (50–60%) when the file must be as small as possible — for a slow connection, an email attachment limit, or sending many photos at once on WhatsApp. Stay higher (90–95%) when the photo will be printed or shown on a large screen and every detail matters. If your form asks for an exact KB size, use the Compress Image to KB tool instead of guessing with the slider.

A worked example

A typical phone photo might be 4,032 × 3,024 pixels and 4.5 MB. Saved again at 80% quality, the same photo usually comes out around 500–700 KB — roughly 85% smaller — and side by side on a screen the two look identical. At 50% quality it may drop below 250 KB, still fine for messaging and web use, though zooming in closely starts to show softness around edges and text.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Compressing an already compressed photo again and again. Each lossy save removes a little more detail. Keep your original safe and compress once, from the original.
  • Going too low for print. A 40%-quality photo looks fine as a thumbnail but disappoints on paper. For printing, stay at 90%+ or use the original.
  • Expecting compression to fix a blurry photo. Compression shrinks files; it cannot add back detail that the camera never captured.
  • Using PNG for photos. PNG is excellent for logos and screenshots but huge for photos. This tool outputs JPG, the right format for photographs.

Private by design

Your image is processed on your own device, inside your browser. Nothing is uploaded, stored or shared — you can even use the tool with your connection switched off once the page has loaded. Re-saving a photo here also strips the hidden EXIF metadata phone cameras embed in files — such as the GPS location where the photo was taken — which is one more reason to compress a copy before posting a photo publicly.

Which quality should you choose? A quick guide

Websites and blogs (90%): images sit next to text and are looked at closely; 90% keeps them crisp at a fraction of the original weight. WhatsApp and messaging (80%): the apps compress again on sending anyway, so 80% loses nothing the recipient would ever see. Email attachments (70–80%): most inboxes cap attachments around 20–25MB; a handful of phone photos at 80% fit comfortably. Online forms (use the other tool): the moment a form names an exact size — under 50KB, 100KB, 200KB — switch to Compress Image to KB, which targets that number precisely instead of leaving you to guess with the slider. That is the standing difference between the two tools: this one is your choice of quality, the other is one exact file size.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How much smaller will my image get?

Most phone photos shrink 70–90% at 80% quality with no visible difference. The exact result depends on the photo — detailed scenes like crowds and foliage shrink a little less than smooth ones like skies and walls.

Q: Does compression change the dimensions?

No — this tool keeps the same width and height and only reduces the file size. For an exact KB target, use the Compress Image to KB tool.

Q: Which formats are supported?

JPG and PNG images. The compressed result is always a JPG, which every website and form accepts.

Q: Is it really private?

Yes. The image never leaves your device; the compression runs in your browser.

Q: Why does the slider stop at 95% quality?

Above about 95%, JPG files grow very large again for almost no visible gain. 95% is the practical top for a compressed copy; for a perfect archival copy, keep your original file.

Q: Can I compress many photos at once?

This page handles one photo at a time so it stays fast and simple. It takes only a few seconds per photo, and there is no limit on how many you can do in a row.
