Zakat Calculator — 2.5% on cash, gold, silver and trade goods

What is Zakat?

Zakat is the annual obligatory charity in Islam: 2.5% of qualifying wealth, paid by adult Muslims whose wealth reaches a minimum threshold called nisab and stays there for one full lunar year (hawl). It is not a tax on income — it is a charge on wealth that is held: savings, gold, silver and trade goods. Money you earn and spend within the year is not zakatable; money that sits with you for a year above nisab is. Pick one day in the year — many people pick a day in Ramadan, though any day works — add up your zakatable wealth on that day, and 2.5% of it is your Zakat.

Nisab: the threshold, in gold and in silver

Nisab is traditionally set at 87.48 grams of gold (7.5 tola) or 612.36 grams of silver (52.5 tola). Because silver is far cheaper per gram, the silver nisab in rupees is much lower than the gold nisab — and most scholars in Pakistan recommend the silver standard, precisely because it brings more people into the obligation and more help to the poor. Two reference points for 2026: the State Bank of Pakistan's nisab figure used for bank deductions is Rs 614,760, while a silver-based nisab at a silver price around Rs 530 per gram is 612.36 × 530 = about Rs 324,550. The gap between those two numbers is exactly why the basis matters — and why this calculator lets you choose, defaulting to silver. If you hold only gold and nothing else, the gold nisab applies to you; if your wealth is mixed — cash, gold, silver and goods together — the silver nisab is the standard most scholars apply.

A worked example

Suppose on your Zakat day you hold: cash Rs 400,000; gold 20 grams at Rs 35,500 per gram = Rs 710,000; silver 100 grams at Rs 530 per gram = Rs 53,000; trade goods worth Rs 0; and short-term debts of Rs 50,000. Total zakatable wealth = 400,000 + 710,000 + 53,000 − 50,000 = Rs 1,113,000. Silver nisab = 612.36 × 530 = Rs 324,551. Your wealth is above nisab, so Zakat = 1,113,000 × 2.5% = Rs 27,825. On the gold basis instead, nisab would be 87.48 × 35,500 = Rs 3,105,540, and this same person would owe nothing — which shows, in one example, why the choice of basis changes the answer completely and why you should settle it with a scholar you trust if you are unsure.

What counts — and what does not

Counts: cash in hand and in bank accounts (including digital wallets), gold and silver in any form including jewellery (Hanafi opinion includes jewellery kept for wearing), the market value of shares held for trading, business stock and trade goods, and money owed to you that you expect to receive. Does not count: your home, your car, your clothes, household furniture, and the tools of your trade — things you use, not things you hold as wealth. Salary is a common confusion: a salary is not itself zakatable, but once it is saved and sits in your account for a full lunar year above nisab, it becomes zakatable like any other savings. Long-term debts are another trap: only the amount actually due within the year is deducted, not a whole 20-year loan balance.

Who can receive Zakat?

The Quran (Surah At-Tawbah, verse 60) names eight categories: the poor, the needy, those employed to administer Zakat, those whose hearts are to be reconciled, freeing captives, people in debt, in the cause of Allah, and stranded travellers. The practical rule most people need: give to someone whose own wealth is below nisab. You do not need to investigate them like a detective — if you reasonably know they are not wealthy, that is enough — but you cannot pay Zakat to your own parents, children or spouse, or to someone who is already above nisab, and Zakat cannot be used to pay for a mosque building or a public project that benefits everyone including the wealthy.

Common mistakes

  • Using last year's gold price. Gold and silver move fast; a price from six months ago can shift your Zakat by thousands of rupees. Update the price fields on your Zakat day.
  • Forgetting jewellery. In the Hanafi school followed by most Pakistanis, gold jewellery counts even if it is worn regularly. Other schools differ — another reason to confirm with your own scholar.
  • Counting the whole loan. Only short-term, currently payable debts are deducted. Counting a full mortgage balance can wrongly wipe out a real obligation.
  • Paying on income instead of wealth. Zakat is 2.5% of what you hold on your Zakat day, not 2.5% of what you earned in the year.
  • Waiting for Ramadan out of habit. Ramadan is a blessed time to pay, but if your hawl completes in another month, that month is when it is due.

A note on bank deductions in Pakistan

Under Pakistani law, banks may deduct Zakat at source from certain accounts on the first of Ramadan, subject to nisab and to a declaration (on Form CZ-50) if you wish to opt out and pay privately. If your bank has already deducted Zakat on an account, do not count that same money again in your own calculation — deduct what was taken from the wealth you assess, or treat the bank's deduction as part of what you paid. Keep the bank's advice slip with your own record so the same wealth is never zakated twice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How much is Zakat?

2.5% of your total zakatable wealth, once that wealth reaches nisab and stays there for one lunar year.

Q: What is nisab?

The minimum wealth threshold: 87.48 grams of gold or 612.36 grams of silver. Scholars in Pakistan commonly use the silver nisab, which is lower in rupees — this calculator defaults to it and lets you switch to gold.

Q: Is Zakat due on my salary?

Not on the salary itself, but on what you save from it: once saved money stays with you for a full lunar year above nisab, it becomes zakatable like any other savings.

Q: Does my gold jewellery count?

In the Hanafi school followed by most Pakistanis, yes — gold jewellery counts even when worn regularly. Other schools differ, so confirm with a scholar you trust.

Q: Can I deduct my full home loan?

No. Only the amount actually due within the year is deducted. Counting a whole long-term loan balance would wrongly erase a real obligation.

Q: My bank already deducted Zakat. Do I pay again?

No — never zakat the same wealth twice. Subtract what the bank took from your assessment, or count the bank deduction as part of what you paid, and keep the bank slip as your record.
