How EMI Is Calculated: The Formula, Explained with a Worked Example

Every bike loan, car loan and personal loan in the world is priced by the same formula, yet most borrowers only ever hear one number from it — "your monthly installment will be…" This guide opens the formula up: what EMI means, how the maths works step by step, and how to use it to compare two loan offers honestly before you sign anything.

What EMI actually is

EMI stands for Equated Monthly Installment: the fixed amount you pay the bank every month until the loan ends. "Fixed" describes the payment, not its contents. Every EMI is a mixture of two things — interest (the bank's charge for that month, calculated on what you still owe) and principal (a piece of the amount you borrowed, paid back). In the first months, when you owe the most, the interest part is large and the principal part is small. Month by month the balance falls, so the interest part shrinks and the principal part grows — the payment stays identical while the loan quietly accelerates towards zero. This system is called reducing balance, because interest is charged only on the balance that remains.

The formula

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1)

  • P = the amount you borrow (the principal)
  • r = the monthly interest rate = yearly rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100
  • n = the number of monthly payments

If the interest rate is zero, there is nothing to calculate: EMI is simply P ÷ n. The formula exists because of the reducing balance — it finds the one fixed payment that exactly clears the loan, interest included, on the final month.

A full worked example

You borrow 500,000 at 18% per year for 3 years (36 months).

  1. Monthly rate: r = 18 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.015
  2. (1 + r)n = 1.01536 ≈ 1.7091
  3. EMI = 500,000 × 0.015 × 1.7091 ÷ (1.7091 − 1) = 500,000 × 0.015 × 1.7091 ÷ 0.7091 ≈ 18,076 per month
  4. Total paid over the loan: 18,076 × 36 ≈ 650,700
  5. Total interest: 650,700 − 500,000 ≈ 150,700 — nearly a third of what you borrowed, on top of it.

Why duration matters more than the monthly payment

Keep the same 500,000 at 18% but stretch it to 5 years (60 months). The formula now gives an EMI of about 12,697 — the monthly payment falls by roughly 5,400, which feels like a relief. But total interest climbs to about 261,800. The longer loan costs about 111,000 more for the identical purchase. This trade-off is the single most important thing the formula teaches: a smaller EMI is not a cheaper loan; it is usually the opposite. Choose the shortest duration whose EMI your monthly budget can carry safely, and be suspicious of any seller who only ever talks about the monthly figure.

What the first payments really do

On that 3-year example, the first month's interest is 500,000 × 0.015 = 7,500. Of your 18,076 payment, only 10,576 reduces the loan. By the last month, the balance is so small that interest is only about 267 and almost the whole payment is principal. Two practical consequences: first, settling a loan very early saves less interest than people expect, because most of the interest is scheduled early. Second, any extra payment you make goes straight to principal and removes interest from every remaining month — the most powerful move a borrower has, if the bank applies it immediately and charges no penalty. Always ask about early-settlement terms before signing.

Flat rate vs reducing balance — the expensive confusion

Some dealers and informal lenders quote a flat rate: interest charged on the original amount for the whole term, even as you pay it back. A "12% flat" loan on 500,000 for 3 years charges 180,000 interest (12% × 500,000 × 3), and your monthly payment is (500,000 + 180,000) ÷ 36 = 18,889 — more than the 18% reducing-balance loan above, despite the "lower" rate. As a rough rule, a flat rate costs about the same as a reducing rate nearly twice as high. Before comparing any two offers, ask one question: "Is this rate reducing balance or flat?" If the seller cannot or will not answer, walk away.

What the formula does not include

The EMI formula covers principal and interest only. Real loans can add processing fees, documentation charges, forced insurance and late-payment penalties — none of which appear in the EMI. When comparing offers, compare the total amount payable including every fee, and read the early-settlement clause. The three checks before you sign

  1. Calculate the EMI and total interest for the exact amount, rate and duration offered.
  2. Run the same amount at a shorter duration and see how much interest disappears.
  3. Confirm the rate type (reducing vs flat), the fees, and the early-settlement penalty — in writing.
